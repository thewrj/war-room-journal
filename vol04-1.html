The Seat That Survived 2008: How the NNP Lost St. Mark

St. Mark stayed with the NNP when the party was routed in 2008, and again when it lost power in 2022. In 2026, with a new candidate on the ballot, its two-party share fell 14 points, faster than the country as a whole, and the NDC took the seat by 261 votes.

St. Mark is Grenada's smallest parish and one of its most loyal constituencies. When the National Democratic Congress swept 11 of 15 seats in 2008, St. Mark stayed with the New National Party. When the NDC took government in 2022, St. Mark stayed again, giving Clarice Modeste-Curwen 1,470 votes to 995, almost 60 per cent of the two-party vote. It was one of only four NNP seats in 2008 and one of six in 2022.

"A loyal seat is loyal to someone. When she left the ballot, the vote fell faster than the national swing."Greg Hosten

On 5th November that record came to an end. The NDC's Caris Degannes took 1,538 votes to 1,277 for the NNP's Mayanna Charles, a majority of 261. A seat that had withstood two NDC waves did not withstand the third.

A loyal seat, a new name

Ms. Modeste-Curwen, a former Minister of Health, Works and Tourism, had represented St. Mark through good years and bad, but she was not on the 2026 ballot. The NNP's candidate was Mayanna Charles, standing in a seat where the party's support had long rested heavily on its sitting member.

The NDC treated St. Mark as a prize from the outset. When Prime Minister Dickon Mitchell announced the election date in October, he named St. Mark in his own remarks as a place the party intended to win. A governing party does not single out a seat it expects to lose.

Faster than the national swing

The scale of the fall is the first thing to grasp. Between 2022 and 2026 the NNP's share of the two-party vote in St. Mark dropped from 59.6 per cent to 45.4 per cent, a fall of 14.3 points, against a national decline of 10.3 points. St. Mark did not merely follow the national tide; it ran four points ahead of it against the NNP.

Compare that with St. George North West, where Dr. Keith Mitchell handed over to a successor he had named in public ten months before polling day, and where the NNP's share fell by just 2.4 points. The two seats offer the clearest test in this series of what happens when a long-serving NNP member leaves the ballot: in one the vote held, while in the other it fell faster than the national swing.

New voters, and nearly all for the NDC

The composition of the change matters. The NNP lost 193 votes, from 1,470 to 1,277, while the NDC gained 543, from 995 to 1,538. Even if every one of the NNP's lost voters had switched, at least 350 of the NDC's new votes came from people who had not voted for either main party in 2022.

The register in St. Mark grew by 510 names, 329 more ballots were cast, and turnout held almost exactly steady, at 67.3 per cent. The NDC, in other words, did not win St. Mark by persuading NNP voters; it won by reaching the new ones first.

Not a single stronghold left

The polling-division returns show how evenly the NDC spread its gains. The NNP carried only 3 of the 13 divisions and tied a fourth: at the Victoria Pre-Primary School, one division split exactly 106 votes each.

The NNP's best ground was the St. Mark's Secondary School, where it took 51.3 per cent across three divisions and won one by 131 to 104, together with one of the two Duquesne divisions, which it won by 118 to 112. Its worst was Samaritan, where the NDC won both divisions by 210 votes to 97 between them; in one Samaritan division the NNP took barely more than a quarter of the two-party vote.

Victoria, the parish capital, was lost narrowly. The NDC took the two Victoria Child Development Centre divisions by 287 votes to 213, and the three Pre-Primary divisions by 335 to 316. The margins were small everywhere, and all of them ran the same way.
