The Third Force: How the DPM Could Overtake the NNP

The Democratic People's Movement won 1,189 votes in its first election, 21,378 behind the NNP, and beat it in just one seat. New parties have risen quickly in Grenada before, but only after an older party had fallen apart. This volume maps the DPM's only realistic route to second place.

GH
By Greg Hosten4th March 2027 · 16 min read
WhatsAppXFacebook

Every party in Grenada must eventually answer the same question: what happens after its founder? The NNP took two years too long to answer it, and paid the price in 2026. For the DPM the question arrives early, because so much of what it won came from one man.

Will Mr. David stand again?

Mr. David was born in 1957. By 2030/31 he will be in his mid-seventies, and he has represented the Town in four parliaments since 2003. At the time of writing, he has not said publicly whether he will lead the DPM into another election.

His personal standing is not in doubt. Before the election, polling attributed to Peter Wickham and discussed on a local radio programme gave him a rating of 6.7 against 5.3 for his NDC opponent, yet he still lost by 539 votes. A strong personal rating did not survive a national wave, and that single result encapsulates the DPM's dilemma.

If Mr. David stands again, the DPM retains its only proven vote-winner but risks remaining a one-seat, one-person party. If he does not, it loses the 730 votes that made up most of its total, unless someone else can inherit them. Either way, the decision cannot be deferred until the writ.

A successor, mentored in time

The best example of a handover in this series is St. George North West, where Dr. Keith Mitchell named Dr. Adrian Joseph ten months before polling day and campaigned beside him, and the NNP's share fell by only 2.4 points against a national swing of 10.3. Where the NNP left succession to chance, as in St. Mark and St. George South East, it fell much further.

If the DPM is to outlast its founder, Mr. David should name a successor, or a small leadership team, by the end of 2028, leaving two years for the new leader to become known before an election that could come early. The successor should be given substantive work from the outset: a national portfolio such as the cost of living, a target seat to build, and a place beside Mr. David in the Town, walking the Stadium divisions that gave the party 454 votes.

The test of a worthy successor is not loyalty but whether that person can win votes the founder cannot: younger voters, voters outside the capital, and NNP supporters who would never vote for Mr. David after the split of 2024. The party's deputy leader, Arley Gill, is the obvious first candidate, but he won 82 votes in St. John. Whoever is chosen will have to build a base of their own, and the founder's task is to make room for it.

Two successions, not one

Mr. David holds two positions that the DPM will eventually need to fill: the party's candidate in the Town of St. George, and its political leader. They are different jobs, they call for different qualities, and they need not be filled by the same person. Treating them as a single decision is the most common way founder-led parties get succession wrong.

The Town requires someone the capital already knows, who can hold the 730 votes Mr. David won and find the 286 more that would bring the party to 40 per cent. The leadership requires someone who can speak for the DPM in every constituency, raise money, recruit candidates and hold the movement together. A candidate who is excellent at one may be poorly suited to the other.

A successor for the Town of St. George

The Town is the one seat the DPM can realistically win, and Mr. David's vote there was personal, built over twenty years. A successor will inherit it only if voters see the handover happen in front of them. The best candidate is someone who lives in the constituency and is already known in it: a councillor, a church or community leader, a small-business owner on the Carenage or a professional raised in the Stadium divisions, rather than a newcomer brought in from elsewhere. Vol. 1 showed what happened to the NNP when it chose a candidate who lived outside the seat.

The method is the one that held St. George North West for the NNP in 2026. Mr. David should name his preferred Town candidate publicly, ideally in 2027, with the branch endorsing the choice. For the next three years the two should walk the twelve polling divisions together, beginning with the five National Stadium divisions that gave the DPM 454 votes and the older town divisions at Marryshow House and the SDA School, where the margins were smallest. The successor should take over constituency casework and community events while Mr. David is still in public life, so that voters come to the new candidate for help before they are asked for a vote.

Success can be measured. By 2029 the candidate should have a household list covering every division, a captain in each of the twelve, and recognition in independent polling close to Mr. David's own. On polling day the target is simple: hold the 730, and reach the 1,016 votes that would have given the DPM 40 per cent of the Town in 2026.

A successor for the party leadership

The leadership is a national job and should be filled by a national process. The DPM elected Mr. David as political leader by a unanimous delegate vote at its first congress in March 2026; his successor should be chosen the same way, by delegates at a congress, in an open contest with published rules. The NNP's 2024 convention, held two years late and ending with the losing candidate alleging that it was rigged, is the precedent to avoid. A leadership that delegates have chosen freely is far harder for opponents, or disappointed rivals, to challenge later.

Mr. David can still shape the outcome without dictating it. He can identify two or three potential leaders early, give each of them a visible national role, such as spokesperson on the cost of living, on youth employment or on the diaspora, and let members judge them on their work over two years. The deputy leader, Arley Gill, is the natural starting point, but an open field gives the party a real choice and gives the eventual winner real authority. The congress should be held by the end of 2028, so that the new leader has at least two years before an election that could come early.

Once the vote is held, the most important thing Mr. David can do is accept it in public and campaign for the winner, whoever that is. A founder's endorsement after a fair contest is worth more than any endorsement before one.

One person or two?

There is a case for combining the two roles. A party leader outside parliament has no platform in the House, as the NNP discovered when its own leader lost her seat in 2026, and the Town is the DPM's only seat within reach. If the new leader is also a credible candidate in the capital, standing there gives the party its best chance of a parliamentary voice.

The risk is concentration. If the leader stands in the Town and loses, the party loses its seat and its leader's standing in a single night, as the NNP did in St. Andrew South East. The safer arrangement for a small party is often to separate the roles: a rooted local candidate in the Town, and a leader who builds a second target seat, so that the DPM has two routes into parliament rather than one.

Whichever model the DPM chooses, three conditions decide whether it succeeds: both successors are named early enough to become known, both are chosen through processes the party's members accept as fair, and Mr. David visibly hands over rather than holding on. A founder who manages both successions well can leave behind a party. One who leaves them to chance leaves behind a memory.
